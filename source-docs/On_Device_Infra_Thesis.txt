On Device Infra Thesis

“The emergence of agentic AI workloads, to have OpenClaw as an early example, are fundamentally changing user experiences across connected edge devices and reshaping our roadmap in every platform we develop.” - Cristian Amon, Qualcomm Q2 2026 earnings call

GPT 3.5, a 175B parameter model for example, ran on large amounts of A100 GPUs. Google's Gemma 4, released this month, runs a natively multimodal, reasoning capable model with up to 31B parameters, and quantized versions built to fit limited memory architecture (Gemma 4 Technical Report). Benchmark capabilities have grown on edge devices, but tooling to manage, deploy, and update those capabilities has not. Meanwhile, compression once looked solved, but reasoning models broke it, because the predictable accuracy-for-size tradeoff no longer holds. The use cases for small models and edge AI can be grouped into two broad categories: 1) where a product already has a computer on board that a small model can run on (e.g. cars)  2) net new products that are enabled by edge AI devices (e.g. embodied AI systems).

The gap exists because edge hardware does not compare to a datacenter. Researchers have built small models that function on less hardware, but the challenge is preserving capabilities of frontier models.

This thesis makes three claims.

Research that once limited edge AI is mostly solved or on a viable path to a solution, which shifts the constraint from model capability to operations

Value will accrue to two layers: edge fleet control, the operational layer that keeps deployed devices functional and observable, and new compression methods that maintain reasoning ability. These methods have been published by independent researchers at universities who later go on to found companies as well as leading labs such as Deepmind and Deepseek.

The buyers are enterprises and governments running hardware in industrial, medical, defense, or robotic settings where offline operation is essential

What is edge AI and why does it matter?

Edge AI is any AI system that runs on local hardware, smartphones, sensors, wearables, controllers, etc., rather than on a remote server, which is how frontier models like Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini are served today. The distinction matters for four reasons:

Latency. Inference occurs on device, so requests that take hundreds of milliseconds collapse to single digits. For intended use cases such as tool calling or specific workflows on device inference is very fast, but very complex tasks with large input/output will run slower on device, limited by hardware, etc.

Offline operation. The model comes embedded in the hardware, so it keeps working without a network connection, which matters anywhere connectivity is unreliable or deliberately unavailable.

Privacy. Data never leaves the device, which matters in regulated industries and for any company wary of sending its data across a network it doesn't control.

Cost structure. Spend shifts from a recurring, usage-based inference bill to an upfront hardware purchase. Whether that is cheaper depends on the customer. A company running a huge number of inferences per device recoups the upfront cost fast, while a company with light, sporadic usage may never recover it.

How did edge capabilities improve?

This list could run much longer, but I am sharing six important research areas that, stacked on top of each other, brought edge AI to its current capabilities.

Compact architecture: SqueezeNet proved in 2016 that accuracy and parameter count could decouple. Rather than shrinking large models, researchers designed small models that outperformed what their parameter count would imply. These made deep networks feasible on FPGAs and other memory constrained hardware in robotics, medical devices, and satellites

Compression: Quantization rounds weights to lower precision, pruning eliminates unuseful model information and distillation trains small models to mimic large ones. The Deep Compression work showed that these techniques stack multiplicatively with compact architecture, and follow-on research proved the stack transfers across modalities including speech, beyond language models

The runtime layer: TVM made models portable across hardware targets, and TensorFlow and Lite Micro brought inference to microcontrollers. Then new constructs like llama.ccp and Ollama ran quantized models, without custom engineering. TVM's authors productized their research, spinning out as OctoML, which pivoted toward generative AI infrastructure as OctoAI and sold to Nvidia in September 2024 for a reported $250 million

NPUs and per watt performance: Phones, for example, include dedicated AI chips that drain very little battery. Apple integrates its own neural engines into custom systems on chips (SoCs), and a competitive market of standalone players such as Hailo, Axelera, Sima, Kneron, Syntiant, EdgeCortix, Blaize, BrainChip, Innatera, etc., continues to push the frontier of performance per watt. By owning both the chip and the software eligible to run on it, Apple has limited the opportunity for these competitors. Qualcomm, the top chip supplier for Android devices, is racing to build the same hardware-plus-software stack from the chip side. In their Q2 2026 earnings call, Qualcomm CEO Cristiano Amon said "power efficient NPU for local models will be key assets to delivering agentic AI." NPUs matter for on-device AI systems because they offload neural network calculations from CPUs and GPUs, which delivers power efficiency, stronger data privacy, and zero-latency offline performance.

System algorithm design: MCUNet ran frontier image recognition scale inference on microcontrollers by designing the model and deployment infrastructure together. This moved the field from TinyML, for simple classification, to TinyDL, deep models on constrained hardware, through quantization and hardware-aware architectures. Qualcomm acquired Edge Impulse, previously valued at $230M, in 2021, which vertically integrated this space to chip makers.

Capability density: Improved data curation and distillation from frontier models produced Gemma, Qwen, and small Llama models that perform strongly relative to their parameter counts. Alternative architectures add another lever. liquid time-constant networks showed continuous-time recurrent networks matching larger models on time-series tasks with a fraction of the neurons, and state space models like Mamba process large inputs without traditional memory requirements becoming problematic. These matter for devices where a transformer's attention cache, for example, exhausts available RAM. Google's Gemma 4 sits at the front of this domain today. It shares a cache across layers to cut memory and compute, and its mobile quantization scheme targets the deployment problem I cover below. But first:

Why reasoning is hard on a single device

Language models generate one token at a time, and each token depends on the one before it, so the output does not naturally parallelize. A reasoning model that thinks for thousands of tokens before answering compounds this challenge. On a datacenter GPU cluster, providers hide the cost by splitting prefill, reading the prompt, from decode, generating tokens, across separate pools of machines and batching many users' requests together, so the hardware never is idle waiting on a single user's next token. An edge device serving one person at a time cannot batch this way, so the same thinking methods that work in a datacenter run slowly on the device.

The published fixes, speculative decoding (a small draft model proposes several tokens that a larger model verifies in parallel), prefix caching/KV transfer (reusing key-value computations across repeated context instead of recomputing them), sparse-to-dense decoding, and lookahead decoding, are shipping in production inference today. My take is that this layer is a weaker standalone venture opportunity: these techniques are published quickly, absorbed into open source runtimes, and become table stakes rather than a moat. The likely outcome mirrors what happened to the runtime layer itself, a useful wedge that ends up as a feature of the deployment platforms described below rather than a company. I have accordingly treated it as background for this thesis rather than as a fourth category with its own company table.

With the technical background in place, the next question is where a standalone company can capture value. Three approaches look investable to me.

Opportunities

Three opportunities look investable, each covering a different part of the deployment stack:

Reasoning-preserving compression (the model layer)

Compression was a previously solved problem. Using a combination of quantization, pruning, and distillation a model’s performance degraded in a predictable way and teams made known tradeoffs of accuracy for size and speed. Reasoning models upended this philosophy, because performance degradation compounds across the model’s reasoning chain. This problem remains unsolved, and acquirers have already bought compression startups such as Neural Magic (acq. by Redhat in January 2025) and OctoML (acq. by Nvidia for $250M in September 2024). These were not massive outcomes, but they prove the problem matters to frontier companies willing to acquire the teams solving it. The outcomes for companies currently in this space most likely end up similar in size, but the potential set of acquirers is larger today which could yield a higher price. As investors at an early stage it would be wise to be price sensitive given the likelihood of early acquisition.

Researchers explore three solution categories to resolve this:

Protect weights that carry important reasoning signals. Standard compression methods often fail on reasoning models because they focus on short text prompts rather than the model's inner decoding path. To solve this, Reasoning-Aware Compression (RAC) collects signals from the model's actual self-generated chain-of-thought traces, preventing compressed models from "rambling" or losing logic. Similarly, Causal Attribution Pruning (CAP) maps critical paths by masking attention heads during small logic tests to isolate and protect the weights with the highest causal impact on multi-step deduction. Studies analyzing Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) find that logic is highly concentrated in the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) of the final layers. By protecting as little as 2% of these weights from over-compression, developers preserve the model's full analytical capacity at a fraction of the original size.

Compressing the reasoning trace itself. Reasoning Path Compression measures the semantic sparsity of a chain of thought, how much a reasoning trace repeats itself, checks its own work, or re-derives the same intermediate step, and periodically removes unnecessary information from the KV cache. This reduces cache size by up to 75% and lifts throughput by 1.6x, with only a 1.2 point accuracy decrease. A second approach, step entropy, scores each reasoning step by information content and removes low-entropy filler steps keeping only the high-value ones.

Fixing specific failure modes that compression introduces. Quantizing a reasoning model can make it overthink: researchers at Meta FAIR found that in up to 52% of a quantized model's wrong answers, the model reached the right answer at some intermediate step and then talked itself out of it. The leading fixes stay intuitive, adding a small penalty mechanism when models output tokens such as "but" or "wait" that mark the model as second guessing itself. This cut chain of thought length by up to 28% with negligible impact on accuracy.

Gemma 4 from Google combines these research trends. Its mobile quantization scheme applies targeted precision to the layers that generate tokens while holding the model's core reasoning at higher precision. Enterprise willingness to pay can be projected through two lenses. The first is measurable today. A compression vendor that lets customers complete the same work at lower inference cost has a clean, quantifiable pitch. The second is earlier but larger in my opinion. Capable small models unlock edge workflows that cloud model size and economics prohibit, expanding both what customers can automate and the speed at which they operate. (Appendix 2)

Edge fleet control and observability (owning the deployment layer)

Managing thousands of deployed devices with versioning, model tracking, and observability is a distinct problem from building the small model itself. Leading companies in this space follow a similar production diagram, delivering models with SDKs, providing over the air updates with rollback capability, telemetry that respects privacy constraints, opportunistic routing between on-device and cloud inference, and fallback controls for devices under memory or overheating pressure. This playbook resembles Datadog, which capitalized on a wedge of monitoring existing servers and databases. Fleets of edge devices are a new set of tooling that requires similar infrastructure for key users.

Two distinct categories of companies converge on this opportunity from different starting points. One is legacy device fleet management that began with general IoT and hardware operations (OTA updates, remote debugging, teleoperation) before AI models emerged as an important component. The other is AI native, with strength in ML, quantizing models for specific chips, catching quality regressions fast, and routing queries between device and cloud models, but without the traditional operational expertise and coinciding enterprise relationships.

These two groups will converge. Fleet operations without model expertise, and model operations without fleet oversight, each solve half the customer's problem. Whichever side absorbs the other's advantage most effectively will own this layer. The model native group holds the more durable advantage. Model versioning, quality detection, routing, and compression are ML problems that require novel solutions, while OTA delivery and device preparation and configuration, though not trivial, follow standard conventions that an emergent competitor can absorb. That said, adoption may depend on the end market. In dynamic, unstructured domains like physical AI, model first deployment and observability companies have relatively more important features. In repeatable workflows that run on existing models, manufacturing being the clearest example, the incumbent fleet operators are positioned to lead. The pricing models are very similar between the two groups as well - with many offering an open source or free tier with additional per device, per usage, or enterprise subscriptions.

What do Apple/Samsung (and Qualcomm) do here?

Since 2017 Apple has run a vertically integrated stack from its own silicon to developer protocols. A platform owner that controls the chip, OS, and developer distribution channel may well resist a third party solving for model versioning and OTA updates. Apple commands over half of US smartphone share, and any on-device inference layer for iOS is a feature Apple can absorb into Core ML. However, Apple has shown less relative interest in serving as a vendor for other people's devices such as industrial sensors, medical devices, or robots that run on Android, Linux, or microcontrollers, which are the largest volume form factors of edge AI devices.

Qualcomm poses a more direct threat to independent companies in this space. Its acquisitions of Foundries.io, Edge Impulse, and Nexa AI have moved it up the deployment stack, from silicon into fleet tooling and model operations. Unlike Apple, Qualcomm sells into the fragmented device landscape.  A key question for founders in this space will be how an independent player earns the right to exist as an independent player with Qualcomm and others bringing the same tooling in house. (Appendix 3, Appendix 4)

My opinion here is that independent companies can survive on chip fragmentation, serving mixed fleets across Jetson, Apple, MediaTek, etc. hardware.

Small and micro foundation model providers (owns the underlying model)

The first two approaches were tooling, taking existing models and making them run better on limited hardware. This third group builds models from scratch specifically for edge devices, rather than compressing frontier models. The competition here is well defined and includes not just other startups but also the frontier labs' small models such as Nvidia Nemotron, Phi, Gemma, and Qwen, which improve every few months.

A closer look at Liquid AI

Liquid AI justifies a deeper dive given its customer traction and distinct approach to model development. Built on proprietary liquid time constant networks rather than a standard transformer, Liquid has raised $293M in total at a valuation over $2B since its founding in late 2023. Two capabilities distinguish Liquid AI from its competitors. First, its LFM 2.5-230M model outscored Qwen's 800M and Gemma's 1B parameter models on data extraction benchmarks. Second, its go to market function has closed substantial deals with customers such as AMD and Mercedes Benz, who build on top of the LFM models for their in-car intelligence systems. Notably from conversation with a research scientist they have a deal to be announced soon with BMW (my best guess from his description as a three letter large car company).

Liquid presents a unique, appealing opportunity as a leading candidate to emerge as a standalone winner from this category, given its technical differentiation and OEM distribution beyond simple benchmark performance. This would classify as an opportunity fund / growth investment. (Appendix 5)

Conclusion

As noted above, the three categories I believe are investible within edge AI infrastructure today sit in the model compression, fleet control, and small foundation model verticals. Within each category one company interests me most, though I would welcome a conversation with any of them:

General Instinct: their Instinct Edge pipeline takes frontier models and returns a compressed offline runtime for edge hardware given target latency budgets, and their published methodology compressed a frontier model to 3 bits on a single 8GB GPU by protecting a small "6%" of the always active parameter path, similar to the 2%-MLP finding noted earlier. Their compressed model outperforms Gemma 4 on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks. Given the Neural Magic and OctoML acquisitions in this category, with a relatively bull case outcome near $300M, an entry price below $30M would provide realistic opportunity for a 10x ROI.

Cactus Compute: their open-source engine (5k+ stars, an SDK covering iOS, Android, macOS, and wearables) already handles routing and fallback in production while claiming 5x cost savings at sub 120ms latency. Unlike pure fleet operations vendors, they also publish model layer research. Their TurboQuant-H compressed Gemma 4 by 4x with no measurable performance loss, which unlocks new device categories for deployment with improved speed, battery life, and memory headroom. I believe model-native fleet management companies hold a structural advantage with edge AI devices, and Cactus is well positioned to add fleet versioning and OTA updates onto its installed open source base.

Liquid AI: as highlighted above, Liquid is a small model player that has proven both architectural differentiation and go to market success. Against a competitive set of frontier labs whose small models are secondary offerings and compete mainly on quarterly benchmark performance, Liquid's combination of technical differentiation and OEM partnerships has derisked the company. Liquid is a credible category winner that justifies its current valuation.

Common misconceptions

Edge AI is not 100% secure While hackers and data breaches threaten cloud data, adversaries who physically seize an edge AI device can steal it and extract the data stored on it.

Edge devices are not always cheaper than cloud models. Local processing cuts inference costs, but the edge AI devices themselves carry upfront hardware investments that can add nonnegligible costs. Maintaining consistent software, versions, and a fleet of distributed devices is a complex task, and one where companies have been emerging to help.

Edge AI devices can run both cloud and local models. A core benefit for edge AI devices is that they are able to run AI on devices without data ever leaving which comes with a number of benefits including speed, privacy, etc. However, when connected to the internet it is entirely possible for edge AI devices to use cloud AI models - hardware permitting.

Edge AI and IoT are related but not the same IoT devices collect and transmit information to the cloud, while edge AI devices are local computers running complex onboard operating systems, with the ability to act on information independently without cloud involvement.

Edge AI applications

Beachhead markets I expand on below include manufacturing, logistics, retail, medical devices, defense, physical AI, automotive systems, and home devices. These can be split into two categories in my mind. Opportunities that already have computers sitting onboard existing systems and can be modernized, which gives small model providers such as Liquid a fast path to market shipping models onto hardware that exists. The other category are markets that have open problems and no incumbent systems, which lets new entrants own devices as well as the underlying infrastructure for model serving and deployment.

On the modernization of the incumbent systems side, three opportunities stand out in manufacturing, logistics, and automotive. All three share that network delays are consequential and offline operation is required, and have computers onboard existing systems today. Automotive has become the beachhead for Liquid AI, having problems small models can solve now. Manufacturing and logistics share the same profile at large scale, which is where small language models can go to market effectively. As shared on past diligence on Realworld, the issue with deployment was that large models did not provide appealing unit economics while small models degraded quality too much. This tradeoff, as apparent from above research, is quickly trending in the right direction as small task specific models become more capable at better cost profiles. One consideration with customer deployments is whether model quality alone is going to be sufficient in limiting per customer deployment effort that would hamper scalability.

Physical AI is the largest opportunity in the no incumbent systems category and is fit to General Instinct's strength compressing large reasoning models onto small hardware. Compression matters most in the most challenging case, humanoids, which must handle unexpected environments and a wide range of tasks that cannot be trained for in advance. Humanoids remain years from broad deployment, so I view them as the expansion market. The entry point is nearer-term embodied systems such as autonomous mobile robots and robotic arms, where the same compression stack applies on shorter timelines. (Appendix 6)

Home devices are the consumer greenfield case, ranging from voice control of a thermostat to a morning news briefing (Echo Statistics). OpenAI has shown it intends to enter the category as well in either late 2026 or early 2027.. Meta previously sought to enter the at home device space when Portal struggled to gain adoption in part due to consumer privacy concerns. Consumer trust in my view will be a durable moat in this category, and it may favor entrants that keep inference on device.

Works on data shaped as networks of connected points, such as molecules, road maps, or social circles. Each point updates its understanding by exchanging information with its neighbors.

Relational and networked data

Multiplies input values by adjustable weights and passes them through interconnected layers to calculate a final score, like a chain of voting committees passing a weighted opinion down the line.

Tabular and structured data

Processes data step by step while feeding its own internal memory back into itself at every turn, like a running notepad updated with each new word or timestamp.

Time-series, audio

Yes, and cheap to run. Has powered offline voice and keyboard features on phones for years, though developers now increasingly favor transformers and liquid networks for new work.

Sensor anomaly detection, time-series forecasting
